Can Carpet Cleaning Be Deducted from a Security Deposit in Oregon?

By Carpet Friend Medford | July 2026 | Jackson County, OR 9 min read

⚠ This post explains Oregon's residential tenancy law in plain terms for educational purposes. It is not legal advice. For specific situations — especially if you are in or anticipating a deposit dispute — consult an Oregon attorney or the Oregon State Bar's lawyer referral service.

In Oregon, a landlord can deduct carpet cleaning costs from a security deposit only if the carpet's condition exceeds normal wear and tear and the lease specifically requires professional cleaning at move-out. If both conditions are met, only the actual, documented cost of cleaning may be deducted — and a dated service receipt is the standard piece of documentation used to support that deduction.

Move-out season in Medford brings the same question from both sides of the lease every year: can a landlord really take carpet cleaning out of my deposit? And on the other side, am I within my rights to deduct for carpet cleaning when a tenant moves out? Our site's FAQ page gives a short answer to this in a few sentences. This post goes further — walking through what Oregon law actually frames, how "normal wear and tear" tends to get interpreted, what makes a deduction legitimate versus improper, and a practical step-by-step guide for both tenants and landlords in Jackson County. None of this replaces advice from a licensed Oregon attorney for your specific situation, but it should give you a much clearer picture of the landscape than a three-sentence answer can.

What Oregon Law Actually Says — ORS 90.300

Legal Framework

Oregon's residential landlord-tenant statute, ORS 90.300, governs security deposits. In general terms, it sets up a framework where deductions from a deposit are meant to cover damage beyond normal wear and tear, not routine upkeep or cosmetic refresh items a landlord would reasonably expect to handle as a cost of doing business. The statute also requires landlords to provide tenants with a written, itemized accounting of any deductions within a set window after the tenant moves out — commonly cited as 31 days.

This is a general summary of the statutory framework, not a substitute for reading the statute itself or consulting an attorney. Oregon law changes periodically, and how a specific clause applies to your lease, your unit, and your circumstances can vary. If you are facing an active dispute, the Oregon Law Center or the Oregon State Bar's lawyer referral service are appropriate next steps — not a blog post.

What does the two-part test actually mean in plain language? Two conditions generally need to be true before a carpet cleaning deduction is proper. First, the condition of the carpet has to exceed normal wear and tear — meaning the carpet shows more than the expected fading, light matting, or minor marks that come from ordinary living over a lease term. Second, the lease itself has to specifically require professional cleaning at move-out. A landlord who has only one of these two elements — a slightly worn carpet but no specific lease clause, or a lease clause but a carpet in genuinely normal condition — is on much shakier ground than a landlord who has both.

The documentation requirement matters just as much as the substantive test. Generally speaking, landlords are expected to provide tenants a written, itemized accounting of any deposit deductions within a set number of days after the tenant vacates the unit — commonly described as 31 days under Oregon practice. This accounting should show what was deducted, why, and typically what it cost. This is the procedural half of the framework: even where a deduction might be substantively justified, failing to document and disclose it properly can create problems for a landlord. Again, for the precise statutory language and how it applies to a specific lease, consult an attorney or refer directly to the Oregon Revised Statutes.

What "Normal Wear and Tear" Means — and How It's Interpreted in Practice

What counts as normal wear and tear on carpet? Normal wear and tear generally refers to the gradual, unavoidable deterioration that happens simply from a tenant living in a unit and using it as intended. Gradual fading from sunlight, light pile matting in high-traffic paths like hallways and in front of couches, and small, unavoidable furniture indentations are the kinds of things typically treated as ordinary wear. Beyond-normal wear generally includes things like pet stains and odor, food or beverage stains, burns, tears or rips, and heavy soiling that reflects neglect rather than ordinary use.

Normal Wear and Tear

  • Gradual, even fading from sunlight over the lease term
  • Light pile matting in high-traffic walkways
  • Minor, unavoidable furniture indentations
  • General "broken-in" look consistent with tenancy length
  • Light soiling consistent with ordinary vacuuming and use

Beyond Normal Wear and Tear

  • Pet stains, pet odor, or urine damage
  • Food or beverage stains not addressed during tenancy
  • Burns, tears, rips, or cuts in the carpet
  • Heavy soiling consistent with neglect, not ordinary use
  • Mold or moisture damage from unreported leaks

How do landlords and tenants typically sort out where a specific carpet falls? In practice, several factors tend to get weighed when a carpet's condition is in a gray area. Length of tenancy matters — a carpet at the end of a five-year lease is expected to show considerably more wear than one at the end of a one-year lease, and a court or mediator would typically expect landlords to factor tenancy length into their expectations. The carpet's age and condition at move-in also matters a great deal, which is why move-in documentation is so important on the landlord side. And the quality of documentation on both ends — move-in photos, move-out photos, any mid-lease inspection notes — tends to carry significant weight in how a dispute actually gets resolved. These are the kinds of practical factors that typically get considered in a deposit dispute, not a rigid legal formula, and outcomes can vary considerably based on the specific facts. For anything beyond general understanding, an attorney familiar with Oregon landlord-tenant law is the right resource.

When a Carpet Cleaning Deduction IS Legal in Oregon

Under what conditions can a landlord actually deduct for carpet cleaning? Generally speaking, three conditions tend to line up when a deduction is on solid footing.

  1. 1

    The carpet's condition exceeds normal wear and tear

    Stains, odor, damage, or soiling beyond what would be expected for ordinary use over the tenancy length — not simply a carpet that looks "lived in."

  2. 2

    The lease specifically requires professional cleaning at move-out

    Generic language like "unit must be returned in clean condition" may not be sufficient on its own to justify a carpet-specific deduction. Landlords should review their lease wording carefully — and ideally have specific carpet-cleaning language reviewed by an attorney — rather than relying on broad, non-specific clauses.

  3. 3

    The deduction reflects the actual, documented cost

    A proper deduction matches what was actually paid for cleaning, supported by a receipt — not a round-number estimate or an amount unrelated to any real invoice.

When a Carpet Cleaning Deduction Is NOT Legal in Oregon

What makes a carpet cleaning deduction improper? On the flip side, several common situations generally point toward a deduction being on weak legal footing.

Carpet was already dirty at move-in, undocumented

Without move-in photos or a condition report, a landlord has little basis to attribute existing wear to the departing tenant.

Condition reflects normal wear and tear for the tenancy length

Ordinary fading or light matting after a multi-year tenancy is generally expected, not a chargeable defect.

The lease doesn't specifically require professional cleaning

A vague "leave the unit clean" clause is generally weaker support for a carpet-specific charge than explicit lease language.

The deduction exceeds the actual documented cleaning cost

Charging a flat, unsupported amount rather than the real invoiced cost is a common point of dispute.

⚠ This list describes commonly disputed scenarios in general terms. Whether a specific deduction is lawful depends on the exact lease language, documentation, and facts involved — a determination best made with an attorney's review, not a blog post.

Guide for Tenants Moving Out in Medford, Oregon

What should a Medford tenant actually do to protect their deposit? Here's a practical, step-by-step approach.

  1. 1

    Read your lease for specific carpet-cleaning language

    Before you assume anything, check whether your lease actually requires professional carpet cleaning at move-out, and what exact wording is used. This shapes everything that follows.

  2. 2

    Document carpet condition at move-out with timestamped photos

    Photograph every room's carpet, including close-ups of any high-traffic areas, before you leave. Timestamped photos are your strongest evidence if a dispute arises later.

  3. 3

    Book a professional cleaner and get a dated receipt

    If your lease requires it, having your own move-out carpet cleaning done proactively, with a dated, itemized receipt, is often the simplest way to satisfy the requirement on your own terms rather than leaving it to the landlord.

  4. 4

    Provide the receipt to your landlord along with your move-out documentation

    Submit the dated receipt together with your photos and any move-out checklist. This creates a clear paper trail showing the requirement was met before you handed back the keys.

  5. 5

    If the landlord still deducts unreasonably

    Oregon's general framework calls for landlords to provide a written, itemized accounting of any deductions within a set window after move-out. If a deduction seems to ignore your documentation or exceed the actual cost, Oregon law provides remedies for tenants when landlords don't follow proper procedure — but the specifics of what remedy applies depend on your facts. Tenants with qualifying incomes can seek help from the Oregon Law Center, and the Oregon State Bar's lawyer referral service can connect anyone with a private attorney for a consultation.

Guide for Landlords and Property Managers in Jackson County

What should a landlord or property manager do to make a carpet cleaning deduction defensible? The same evenhanded logic applies in reverse.

  1. 1

    Check your lease for specific carpet-cleaning language

    Generic "return unit clean" clauses may not be enough to support a carpet-specific deduction. Consider adding explicit carpet-cleaning language to your lease template, reviewed by an attorney, so expectations are clear from day one.

  2. 2

    Document carpet condition at move-in with photos

    A dated set of move-in photos is the single most useful piece of evidence for establishing a baseline condition, and it protects you if a later dispute arises.

  3. 3

    Use a professional cleaner with a dated receipt for baseline and turnover

    Having carpets professionally cleaned between tenants — with a dated receipt kept on file — establishes a clean, documented starting point for the next tenant and supports any future deduction with real, verifiable cost data.

  4. 4

    Apply the two-part test before deducting anything

    Before charging a former tenant, confirm the condition genuinely exceeds normal wear and tear for that tenancy length, and that your lease actually requires professional cleaning. Deduct only the actual documented cost, and provide the written accounting promptly.

  5. 5

    Know the local rental market context

    Jackson County is an active rental corridor with frequent turnover across neighborhoods from White City to Central Point. Consistent, well-documented practices at every turnover reduce disputes and make each move-out easier to handle fairly.

Why the Dated Service Receipt Is the Most Important Document in a Deposit Dispute

Why does a receipt matter so much in a deposit dispute? A dated, professional service receipt does something photos alone can't: it establishes that a specific service actually happened, on a specific date, at a specific address, performed by a specific company, at a specific documented cost. That combination — date, address, provider, scope of work, and price — is exactly the kind of concrete record that supports a deduction on the landlord side, or proves compliance on the tenant side.

What makes a receipt actually useful for this purpose? A receipt that will hold up in a deposit conversation should include the full property address, the date of service, a description of the services performed (carpet cleaning specifically, not a vague "cleaning" line item), the total amount charged, and the company's name and credentials. A receipt from a licensed, insured, IICRC-certified company carries more weight than a generic or informal invoice.

What a receipt alone cannot do is document the condition of the carpet before and after the work. It proves that cleaning happened and what it cost — it doesn't prove what the carpet looked like beforehand. That's why the strongest documentation pairs a dated receipt with timestamped photos taken close to the same date, whether you're a tenant proving you met a lease requirement or a landlord supporting a deduction.
